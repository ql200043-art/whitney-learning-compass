BEFORE
Prepare & Wonder
Build curiosity before the workshop.
Learning objectives
Explain force and motion using an actual workshop observation.
Students will describe one step in an engineering-design process by comparing a prediction with observed
results and explaining a possible reason for any difference.
Classroom preparation
Invite students to recall examples of pushes and pulls and how they can change an objectʼs motion.
Prepare a simple paper-and-pencil recording sheet with columns for trial or setup, prediction, actual
observation, and possible explanation; leave observation fields blank until observations occur.
Review that any catapult use and handling during the program follow museum staff directions.
Background concepts
A force is a push or pull that can change an objectʼs motion.
The catapult activity varies band count and the position of the supplied ping-pong ball; students can compare
what they observe across those changes.
Engineering design involves making a prediction, observing a result, and using evidence to consider what to
change or explain.
Inquiry questions
How might changing the number of bands affect the catapultʼs motion?
How might changing the ping-pong ballʼs position affect what happens?
What evidence would help you compare two setups fairly?
DURING
Explore & Observe
Follow staff directions. Collect evidence, not invented results.
Observation prompts
If museum staff permit, attend to how the catapultʼs motion appears to change when the band count or ball
position changes; record only what is actually observed.
Use a blank table: setup/change | prediction | actual observation | possible explanation. Leave the actual-
observation column empty until the event is observed.
Follow museum staff directions for all catapult handling and procedures; do not adjust or operate equipment
independently.
Hypothesis & prediction questions
Before a staff-directed comparison, what do you predict may happen if the band count changes? Label this as
a prediction, not an observation.
What do you predict may happen if the ballʼs position changes? What makes you think so?
Critical-thinking prompts
Which factor changed between the setups you observed, and what appeared to stay the same?
Does the observed result support your prediction, differ from it, or leave the question open? What specific
observation informs your thinking?
What other factor might affect the motion, and what would you need to observe before drawing a conclusion?
Evidence collection
Record the setup and the directly observed result in words or a simple sketch; do not fill in results before
observing them.
Keep predictions separate from observed evidence, and note when an observation is unavailable or unclear
rather than guessing.
Ask museum staff before recording or comparing any details not included in the staff-directed activity.
Teacher notes & actual observations
Leave observations blank until the visit occurs. Label predictions as predictions; do not treat them as collected
evidence.
No observations recorded. To be completed by the educator after actual observation.
AFTER
Reflect & Extend
Connect actual observations to your learning goals.
Reflection & discussion
Which actual observation most helped you think about force and motion? Explain how it connects to the
setup.
Where did an observation support or challenge your prediction? What evidence led you to that view?
What would you still want to observe before making a stronger claim about how a setup affects motion?
Follow-up classroom activity
On paper, have students choose one observed comparison and make a labeled sketch of the setup, then write
a claim about motion supported by an actual recorded observation. If no observation record is available, use
the blank table to plan what they would look for next; do not supply invented results.
Learning assessment
Objective 1: Review each studentʼs explanation for a connection between a changed setup feature (band
count or ball position) and motion, supported by an actual observation. If none is available, assess the
studentʼs ability to state what evidence would be needed instead.
Objective 2: Check whether the student separates prediction from observed result and uses the observation
to explain a possible outcome or difference; do not score hypothetical outcomes as empirical evidence.
Optional extension
Students can add a paper-and-pencil design reflection: identify one feature they would want to compare next,
state a clearly labeled prediction, and list the actual observation that would be needed to evaluate it.
