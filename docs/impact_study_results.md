# Participant P01 — Exploratory Pilot Usability Study

**Project:** Whitney Learning Compass — From Curriculum Goals to Hands-On Discovery  
**Study Date:** October 23, 2026  
**Participant ID:** P01  
**Participant Role:** Yale School of Management graduate student (proxy user)  
**K–12 Teaching Experience:** None  
**Study Type:** Exploratory, within-participant comparative usability test  
**Sample Size:** One participant

## 1. Study Objective

Evaluate whether Whitney Learning Compass can help a first-time user identify a suitable museum workshop more efficiently than the Eli Whitney Museum & Workshop's official School Programs catalog.

Also collect initial feedback on recommendation clarity, source transparency, the usefulness of AI-generated instructional planning, and potential improvements.

This is an exploratory usability assessment with a graduate-student proxy user, not a professional educator evaluation or evidence of institutional adoption.

## 2. Standardized Teacher Scenario

The participant was asked to assume the role of a fourth-grade teacher planning a hands-on museum workshop.

| Requirement | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Grade | 4 |
| Number of students | 24 |
| Maximum published program-fee budget | $400, assuming USD |
| Maximum workshop duration | 90 minutes |
| Learning objective | Force, motion, and engineering design |
| Expected task outcome | Identify at least one suitable workshop, determine the class program fee, and recognize operational details requiring museum confirmation |

Transportation, meals, and unverified additional charges were outside the published workshop-fee calculation.

## 3. Procedure and Test Order

The participant used the two interfaces in a fixed sequence:

**Condition A — Official Museum Catalog (first)**

The participant searched the museum's official school-program website and selected a workshop based on the provided grade level, educational objective, fee budget, and time limit.

**Condition B — Whitney Learning Compass (second)**

The participant used Whitney Learning Compass to identify and compare relevant workshops under the same scenario and select a suitable program.

After the comparison, the participant also evaluated the AI-generated Before / During / After teaching plan and the museum inquiry-drafting experience.

The comparison was not randomized or counterbalanced. Exposure to museum program information in Condition A may have made Condition B easier or faster.

## 4. Timing and Task Completion

| Measure | Official Museum Catalog | Whitney Learning Compass |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Course-selection task time | 135 seconds | 65 seconds |
| Selected program | Simple Machines | Marble Roller Coaster |
| Grade eligibility | Supported | Supported |
| Published program fee | $14 per student | $14 per student |
| Class program-fee subtotal | $336 | $336 |
| Published workshop duration | 90 minutes | 90 minutes |
| Suitable program identified | Yes | Yes |
| Fee calculation/comparison completed | Yes | Yes |
| Booking/capacity uncertainties considered | Yes | Yes |

**Observed active selection-time difference:** 70 seconds.

**Relative active selection-time reduction:**

(135 − 65) / 135 × 100 = **51.85%**.

The timing covered only the task of identifying a suitable program. It excluded waiting for AI model responses, reviewing the generated instructional plan, and completing the feedback questionnaire.

Therefore, these measurements do not represent complete end-to-end elapsed time or total planning effort.

Both selected programs met the scenario's verifiable grade, published workshop-fee, and duration constraints. Booking availability and capacity were not independently established.

## 5. Participant Ratings

All ratings were collected on a five-point scale.

| Question | Rating |
| --- | --- |
| Ease of finding a suitable program on the official website | 3/5 |
| Ease of finding and comparing programs using Whitney Learning Compass | 4/5 |
| Confidence that the AI-recommended program meets stated requirements | 4/5 |
| Usefulness of the AI-generated Before / During / After learning plan | 3/5 |
| Likelihood of using the tool for a real classroom-planning task | 4/5 |

The ratings indicate greater perceived ease of program discovery and comparison using Whitney Learning Compass, alongside more moderate satisfaction with the generated instructional plan.

These are subjective ratings from one proxy user, not validated teacher-performance metrics.

## 6. Qualitative Findings

### Finding A — Program discovery and comparison were helpful

The participant appreciated having program fees, duration, and unresolved booking-related information presented directly within the comparison workflow.

The participant perceived that the integrated comparison reduced the need to inspect multiple pages and manually assemble program information.

**Implication:** Evidence-grounded program comparison appears to be a useful feature for first-time users.

### Finding B — Generated learning plans were useful as starting drafts, but lacked specificity

The participant regarded the AI-generated instructional plan as relatively generic and better suited to an initial teaching outline than a fully developed classroom lesson.

More detailed activity design, instructional adaptation, and teacher judgment would still be necessary.

**Implication:** The system should position its learning journey as an editable instructional planning aid rather than a complete educator-approved lesson plan.

### Finding C — Open-ended learning-goal entry created uncertainty

The participant found the official website's predefined filtering options easier to understand than having to independently formulate a learning objective or subject-interest description.

The participant suggested incorporating more guided choices, such as predefined subjects, selectable keywords, or structured topic filters, while preserving the ability to customize requirements.

**Implication:** A hybrid input experience combining structured curriculum choices with optional free-text refinement could reduce first-time user effort.

### Finding D — Museum inquiry drafting provided perceived convenience

The participant also valued the automatically prepared museum inquiry draft, perceiving it as a useful starting point that could reduce manual email preparation.

However, no separate email-drafting task time was collected.

**Implication:** Inquiry drafting is a perceived usability benefit, not a quantified time-saving result in this study.

## 7. Overall Assessment

The pilot provides preliminary evidence that Whitney Learning Compass can support a faster and more convenient program-selection experience for one graduate-student proxy user.

Both the official catalog and Compass produced suitable program choices. Compass required 70 fewer seconds of measured active selection time, and its program-discovery usability rating was one point higher.

The participant also identified meaningful limitations: the AI-generated teaching plan was relatively generic, and the open-ended learning-goal input could benefit from more structured guidance.

The findings support further evaluation but do not establish generalized effectiveness among K–12 teachers.

## 8. Study Limitations

1. **Sample size:** Only one participant.
2. **Participant expertise:** The participant was a Yale SOM graduate student without K–12 classroom teaching experience.
3. **Fixed order:** The official museum website was used before Compass, creating potential learning or order effects.
4. **Limited timing scope:** Model waiting time, plan review, and survey completion were excluded.
5. **Catalog coverage:** Compass uses 20 verified programs, whereas the official museum catalog offers a broader selection.
6. **Different selected programs:** Both choices satisfied the verifiable constraints, but their comparative instructional quality was not independently evaluated by an educator.
7. **No classroom outcome measures:** The study did not evaluate student learning, classroom delivery, actual booking, or museum adoption.
8. **No independent time-study replication:** The observed time difference is descriptive and cannot establish a population-level average benefit.

## 9. Recommended Next Steps

- If feasible, conduct a follow-up pilot with another participant, reversing the interface order to better assess potential order effects.
- Observe whether the request for more guided subject selection recurs independently.
- Evaluate whether instructional plans can be made more specific without introducing unsupported official museum facts.
- Seek feedback from a classroom teacher or museum education practitioner when possible.
- Preserve the original participant responses and distinguish observed evidence from researcher interpretation.
